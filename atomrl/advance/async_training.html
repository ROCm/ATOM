Asynchronous Training¶

Synchronous RL alternates: the rollout generates a batch, the trainer trains on it, the new weights go to the rollout, and every GPU waits for the slowest response in between. AtomRL’s Ray controller path can instead give the rollout GPUs of its own and keep it generating while the trainer trains, in two ways modeled on verl:

Mode

Switch

What overlaps

verl counterpart

One-step off-policy

controller.ray.one_step_off_policy: true

Step s+1’s batch generates while step s trains

one_step_off_policy

Fully async

controller.ray.fully_async.enabled: true

Prompt groups stream to the trainer as they finish; generation runs ahead by a budgeted amount

fully_async_policy

Both train on data generated by slightly older weights and rely on the rollout’s own log-probs (calculate_log_probs: true) with rollout correction (TIS) to correct for it.

Requirements¶

  • The Ray controller (controller.ray.enabled: true) with the vLLM rollout (generation_backend: vllm, vllm_cfg.transport: ray_http). Both modes have been run with the FSDP2 training backend.

  • Or the ATOM rollout (generation_backend: atom, atom_cfg.transport: ray_http). Its RCCL weight sync and fully_async.partial_rollout need an ATOM with the RCCL weight receive and the engine’s pause_generation (mode keep): ATOM main since a0221c199, which merged the RCCL receive (ROCm/ATOM#3058) on top of the pause (ROCm/ATOM#3059), and the release image from -261119 on. An older ATOM fails at start with weight_sync.backend: rdma, as it cannot report RDMA support, and is refused partial rollout; run it with weight_sync.backend: shared_folder and partial_rollout: false, and each sync waits for the groups in flight and goes through shared memory (about 33 s for Qwen3-8B). run_dapo.sh turns sleep mode on for MODE=atom* (a colocated rollout frees its memory for training); pass policy.generation.vllm_cfg.enable_sleep_mode=false.

  • A rollout pool of its own: controller.ray.rollout.num_workers > 0, with the actors on the remaining GPUs (controller.ray.actor.num_workers), for example 4 + 4 on one 8-GPU node.

  • vllm_cfg.enable_sleep_mode: false: the rollout never stops between steps.

  • Weight sync over RCCL (an older ATOM aside): weight_sync.backend: rdma. On a single node this is GPU P2P, so set weight_sync.rdma.require_rdma: false; set weight_sync.rdma.interface and weight_sync.rdma.hca to the node’s NIC and HCA either way. A sync of Qwen3-30B-A3B (61 GB in BF16) takes about 2 s.

Setups that cannot overlap are rejected when training starts: both switches on, no separate rollout pool, a sleeping rollout, and for fully async also partial rollout on an ATOM that cannot pause generation, and moe.r3 (routing replay is not carried across a weight sync).

One-step off-policy¶

controller:
  ray:
    one_step_off_policy: true

While step s trains, step s+1’s batch (including DAPO’s filter_groups regeneration rounds) generates on the rollout pool; the weights are synced after every step. Every batch after the first trains one update after the weights that generated it. This hides the training time behind generation, but not the other way round: when generation is the slow part, the gain is small.

How it works¶

A background thread (StreamingRollout in atomrl/trainer/fully_async_rollout.py) keeps prompt groups (one prompt × num_generations) in flight:

  1. Each group goes, whole, to the replica with the fewest groups in flight.

  2. Groups are collected in the order they finish, scored, and filtered one at a time by DAPO’s filter_groups. Kept groups enter a queue tagged with the weight version that started them.

  3. The trainer takes one step’s worth of groups (train_global_batch_size / num_generations), merges them into a batch, and from there trains exactly as the synchronous loop does.

  4. Every K updates the trainer syncs the weights. With partial_rollout, the engines are paused with vLLM’s pause_generation(mode="keep"): requests in flight are preempted and, after resume_generation(), re-prefilled and continued on the new weights, with no resubmission. Without it, the sync waits for the groups in flight to finish.

The budget counts the groups queued from before the sync, the groups kept since it, and the groups in flight weighted by the recent keep rate. Groups dropped by filter_groups do not count, so a run of dropped groups cannot leave the trainer waiting on a rollout that believes it is ahead. The budget is an expected count, not a cap: when more of the groups in flight are kept than the keep rate predicts, a period ends over budget. This shows most at a sync without partial rollout, which waits for every group in flight; in a six-step example 6 smoke at K = 2, s = 1, the first period kept 42 groups against a budget of 32.

Validation runs on the rollout pool. By default the trainer waits for it and new submissions are held back meanwhile. Its length is that of the longest response, so more replicas do not shorten it: on example 6 it takes about 500 s on 4 or 8 replicas.

With overlap_validation, a validation step starts validation on a background thread instead, and training and new submissions go on beside it. Validation requests share the replicas with the training groups. With partial rollout a weight sync freezes them along with the groups, so the long responses finish on newer weights; val/finished_version minus val/started_version counts the syncs a validation ran through. Without partial rollout the next sync waits for validation to finish, since the engine loads weights only when idle. Metrics are logged at the first step after validation finishes, with val/global_step naming the step validated. One validation runs at a time: a due step waits for the one running before it starts its own. The last step validates in the foreground; when it does, the step before waits for the one running, so the two are not logged on the same step.

On resume from a checkpoint the prompt cursor skips the prompts the run has used, estimated as one gen_batch_size round per step. Groups that were queued or in flight at the checkpoint are not saved; the resumed run generates new ones.

What staleness means here¶

  • A group’s staleness is the number of updates between the weights that started it and the step that trains on it. With partial rollout most of its tokens may come from newer weights, so this is an upper bound.

  • staleness_threshold: 0 is not strictly on-policy: groups still generating at a sync, and the extra groups submitted to make up for dropped ones, train one update late. With K > 1 the later updates of a period also reuse the period’s weights, so staleness grows to K − 1 within it.

  • Staleness is therefore not a hard bound. The over-budget period above trained groups 5 updates after the weights that started them (K = 2, s = 1, where a period on budget stays at 3 or below); async/staleness_max shows when it happens.

  • Every token’s rollout log-prob is the probability it was actually sampled with, so TIS corrects the right ratio whatever the staleness.

Tuning¶

  • The rollout keeps the trainer fed when staleness_threshold is about one group’s generation latency divided by the training step time. On example 6 a group takes 90–150 s and a step trains in 20–30 s, so s = 1–2 with partial rollout. async/trainer_wait_s near zero means the rollout keeps up.

  • Without partial rollout the long tail of the groups in flight holds every sync; at s = 0 that is slower than synchronous training.

  • Raise vllm_cfg.max_num_seqs so each replica can keep enough groups in flight; example 6 uses 512 (32 groups of 16 per replica).

  • trigger_parameter_sync_step > 1 syncs less often at the price of staler data; with syncs of about 2 s there is little to save at K = 1.

  • Groups in flight are capped twice, by the budget and by max_inflight_groups; raise max_num_seqs with s, or the cap stays at max_num_seqs // n per replica (see the two-node results).

  • overlap_validation pays off when validation is long and val_steps training steps take longer than one validation; otherwise the next due step waits for the rest of it.

Limitations¶

  • trigger_parameter_sync_step > 1 has been run end to end only in six-step smoke runs (K = 2, with and without partial rollout); the long runs above use K = 1.

  • Per training step, fully async can learn somewhat less than synchronous training. On a longer run outside example 6 (Qwen3-8B, multi-turn tool calls, 150 steps, s = 1, partial rollout) its AIME24 trailed by 0.03–0.07 from step 90 on, while it reached step 150 in 258 min against 640 min. Compare runs at equal wall-clock time as well as at equal steps.

  • The rollout’s queue and in-flight groups are not checkpointed, and the resumed prompt cursor is an estimate (see above).

  • No decoupled PPO: the trainer recomputes the old log-probs with its current weights and TIS corrects against the rollout’s.

  • Rejected with fully async: partial rollout on an ATOM without pause_generation, and MoE routing replay (moe.r3). Untested: the Megatron training backend. The two-node layout has run 30 steps at most.

The async_training section¶

async_training.enabled selects AsyncRLTrainer, an earlier design that is superseded by the modes above: it generates and trains in turn on the same GPUs, and its rollout engine never loads the updated weights. It logs a warning when used; switch to controller.ray.fully_async or controller.ray.one_step_off_policy.
